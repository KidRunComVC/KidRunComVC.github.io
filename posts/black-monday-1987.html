Market history

Black Monday, 19 October 1987

A 22.6 percent day, and the hedge that sold into its own shadow.

KidRunComVC / 6 October 2026 / 4 minute read

On Monday, 19 October 1987, the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 508 points. That was 22.6 percent, in a single session, to a close of 1,738.74. It is still the largest one-day percentage decline in that index. The figure is so blunt that it tends to end the conversation. The interesting part is how a bad day learned to speed up.

A slope, then a cliff

The week had already gone sour. From the close on Tuesday, 13 October, to the close on the 19th, the Dow dropped 769 points, about 31 percent. The presidential task force that studied the break, the Brady Report, starts its account there, not at Monday's opening bell. This was a slope, and then it was a cliff.

It was also not a New York story that the rest of the world read about later. By the time the cash market opened in the United States, markets elsewhere were already in distress. Orders arrived faster than the plumbing of 1987 could print them. For stretches, the number on a screen was a rumor that had not caught up with the floor. Selling that cannot find a bid does not wait its turn.

The hedge that copied itself

The model was calm. The exit was not.

The explanation that took up the most official ink is portfolio insurance. The product was a hedge. As prices fell, you sold stock-index futures, so a portfolio's downside was capped. For one careful institution, that can work like a seatbelt. For a crowd of institutions running the same seatbelt, it is a feedback loop. Futures fall. Arbitrage pulls the cash market along. Prices fall. The model, doing the only thing it was built to do, says sell more.

The Brady Report put real weight on that loop. Later research agreed the loop mattered and refused to give it the whole cast. Portfolio insurers were heavy sellers, especially in the futures pit, and they were not the only sellers in the room. A machine can amplify a move without being the reason anyone was nervous.

The dry grass was ordinary. A bull market had been running since 1982, and prices had come a long way. Interest rates had been rising. People were uneasy about the dollar and a widening trade deficit. None of that, alone, is a recipe for a 22.6 percent day. It is a reason a market can be tipped. The copied hedge is a reason the tip became a tumble. The broader market fell with the Dow. The S&P 500 lost on the order of a fifth of its value in that same session.

What the pipes did next

The next morning, the Federal Reserve said it stood ready to supply liquidity to the financial system. One job, stated plainly. The sentence did not rewind Monday. It told the pipes they would not be left to seize. Afterward, exchanges and regulators moved toward trading halts, so a cascade is not the only speed a market is allowed to have.

People reach for 1929, and the percentage comparison is fair. The drop on the 19th was in the neighborhood of the two brutal sessions at the end of October 1929, put together. The aftermath was not. This decline did not become a depression. Prices recovered over the following months and years. The mark that stayed was institutional: a hedge is a private plan, and a thousand copies of the same private plan are a crowd. Crowds set the next price.

The lesson

A hedge that sells into a falling market can protect one desk. Copy it widely enough and it stops being a hedge. It becomes the exit.

Sources

  • Presidential Task Force on Market Mechanisms, Report (the Brady Report), January 1988. The Dow's 769-point, 31 percent drop from 13 October to 19 October, and the 508-point, 22.6 percent drop on the 19th, are that report's figures. So is the emphasis on portfolio insurance as an amplifier.
  • Federal Reserve History, "Stock Market Crash of 1987," for the one-day record and the central bank's liquidity statement the next morning.
  • The close of 1,738.74 on 19 October 1987 is the published Dow close for that session.
