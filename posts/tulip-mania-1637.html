Tulip Mania, 1637

A real price spike, a market in contracts, and a legend that got a better press agent.

KidRunComVC / 2 October 2026 / 4 minute read

In the cartoon version, the Dutch Republic loses its mind over a flower. A sailor eats a bulb worth a house, mistaking it for an onion. Fortunes vanish. Someone ends up in a canal. The economy falls over. It is a perfect story. The parts that make it perfect are mostly a later invention.

The flower was real, and so was the spike.

A collector's bloom

Tulips came to Europe from the Ottoman world in the sixteenth century. By the early 1600s they were a collector's object in the Dutch Republic. The blooms people bragged about were often "broken": streaked and flamed, not one flat color. Those streaks are now understood as a virus. In 1637 they were just rare, and rare was the point. The name the pamphlets made famous is Semper Augustus.

Trade in the wind

Through the winter of 1636 and into early 1637, a lot of what changed hands was not a bulb you could put in a pocket. The bulbs were in the ground. Buyers and sellers traded contracts for delivery in the spring, when the bulbs could actually be lifted. The Dutch had a word for business done in promises like that: windhandel, trade in the wind.

The bulbs were real. A lot of the money was a promise about May.

Prices for the prized bulbs did get wild. The popular retelling says "the price of a house," and for a few sales that comparison is not a fantasy. The historian Anne Goldgar went through the notary records instead of the jokes. She found receipts up around 5,000 guilders, about what a nice house cost then. She also found that those prices were outliers. On her count, 37 people paid more than 300 guilders for a bulb, roughly a skilled worker's wages for a year. That is a hot market among merchants, growers, and well-connected townsfolk. It is not a whole nation betting the pantry.

The week the bids stopped

The break has a date. A contemporary account puts it in Haarlem on 3 February 1637. An offering came up, and the buyers were not there. The price was cut. Still quiet. Cut again. Still quiet. Over the next days, contract prices collapsed, especially for the ordinary bulbs that had come along for the ride. Delivery was not even due until late spring, so plenty of the damage was unpaid paper. If you had bought and sold a promise since the summer and never expected a bulb, you had been trading a story about spring.

The legend outgrew the market

What followed was messy in a human way, not an apocalyptic one. Traders argued. Cities asked for a rule. The authorities were slow, and a lot of the guidance amounted to this: settle it among yourselves, and try to stay out of court. There was no clean decree that snapped a ruined economy back together, because the economy had not been ruined by a flower. Goldgar could not find a single person she could identify as bankrupted by the tulip crash. The Dutch economy went on being the Dutch economy.

The version with the onion and the canal comes later, pumped up by pamphlets that wanted a moral and by Charles Mackay's 1841 book Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds, which preferred a good disaster to a narrow one. The moral traveled better than the footnotes. It still does.

None of that makes the winter imaginary. A scarce luxury, a contract market with the goods out of sight, and a few furious weeks of repricing: that part happened. The useful question is how large a story you let the price tell.

The lesson

A rare flower can fetch a wild price. The pamphlet afterward will try to sell you the whole country.

Sources

  • Anne Goldgar, Tulipmania: Money, Honor, and Knowledge in the Dutch Golden Age (University of Chicago Press, 2007). The scale of the market, the house-priced outliers, and the missing bankruptcies follow her archival findings.
  • Contemporary Dutch accounts place the failed bids in Haarlem on 3 February 1637.
  • Charles Mackay, Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds (1841), the telling this page is scraping back down to size.
